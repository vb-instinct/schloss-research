# Walter Schloss and his approach

[Home](../README.md) | Previous: [Summary](01-summary.md) | Next: [Glossary](03-glossary.md)

## Who he was

Walter Schloss (1916 to 2012, [obituary](https://www.bostonglobe.com/metro/obituaries/2012/02/21/walter-schloss-superinvestor-praised-warren-buffett/bt5oEjoHJcoc0DrCPuBfeP/story.html)) was an American investor who learned his craft working for Benjamin Graham, the teacher of value investing. He ran an investment partnership from 1956 to 2002. Warren Buffett described him as a "good guy of Wall Street" who took nothing unless his investors made money, worked with almost no staff, never used inside information, and still beat the S&P 500 (the benchmark index of large US companies) by a wide margin over 47 years. Buffett wrote that this could not be put down to luck.

Source: Buffett's remarks, [Warren Buffett on Walter Schloss (PDF)](https://www.bishoprockcap.com/assets/cdn.prod.website-files.com/5b7c186c36ef662dc0a4b05a/67893fe33410891fccb44fa3_Warren%20Buffett%20on%20Walter%20Schloss.pdf). Asked how he approached investing, his son and partner Edwin said: "We try to buy stocks cheap."

## The approach in plain terms

Schloss did not predict markets or chase exciting stories. He looked for companies selling for less than their assets were worth, and he was patient. The principles below are from his own talks and memos in the source library (see [reading list](../sources/corpus-source-ledger.md)).

1. **A share is a piece of a real business.** Compare the price with what the business is conservatively worth.
2. **Start with what the company owns.** Assets change less than profits do. But check for "soft" assets such as goodwill, which may be worth little.
3. **Prefer little or no debt.** His memo says debt should not reach 100% of the owners' equity. Debt can wipe out an apparently cheap company.
4. **Find out why it is cheap.** Look at years of price history, cash flow, profit margins and who owns the shares. A low price is a clue, not proof.
5. **Read the reports.** Annual and quarterly reports, footnotes and shareholder notices. Look for honest managers who treat outside shareholders fairly.
6. **Spread your bets.** He held dozens of companies, up to about 100, because any single one can go wrong.
7. **Be patient.** He typically held shares for about four years. That is a historical habit, not a promise.
8. **Re-check when facts change.** Do not buy more just because the price fell. His records include a value trap: a retailer bought at about half its book value that he sold at a loss.
9. **Sell when the price becomes reasonable.** Not simply because a share has risen.
10. **Keep costs and emotions low.** No borrowing to invest, no tips, no forecasts of the economy.

## What he did NOT have: one fixed formula

It is easy to think Schloss followed a single formula. He did not. Over time he moved from very strict tests (buying below the value of current assets minus all debts) to looser ones (half or two-thirds of book value, and sometimes close to book value).

The numeric cut-offs used in this project are **our own cautious adaptation** and are not rules Schloss published. They are set out in [How to read the numbers](04-reading-the-numbers.md).

Other things that are our choices, not his:

- **Banks, insurers and miners are left out** of the simple screen because their accounts need different analysis. Schloss did own some of these.
- **India and Australia** are markets we added. He generally preferred US companies and was wary of foreign accounting and politics.

## Why use his approach at all?

Because it is simple, checkable and honest about uncertainty. You can see the assets, check the debts and decide for yourself. It also forces the question "what could go wrong?" before "how much could I make?"
